\begin{abstract}
This seminar paper studies explanation quality and computational efficiency for multilingual sentiment analysis across an encoder model (mDeBERTa) and a decoder model (Qwen). We compare five attribution methods---LOO, LIME, SHAP, Marginalization, and PLEX---on English (high-resource), Afrikaans (mid-resource), and Javanese (low-resource) data. We evaluate normalized faithfulness (NAOPC), runtime, and token count; run non-parametric statistical tests; and analyze robustness through coefficient of variation, length sensitivity, and cross-method disagreement. Quantitative results show a consistent faithfulness--efficiency tension: LIME is the most expensive while PLEX and Marginalization offer lower runtime with competitive NAOPC. We then define a case-study pipeline that grounds these aggregate trends in sentence-level divergences by selecting maximum-disagreement samples, re-running attribution, aligning token-level scores, anchoring with model predictions, and interpreting language-specific disagreement patterns.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Explainability for multilingual NLP is challenging because perturbation-based explanations are sensitive to tokenization, language resource levels, and model architecture. In sentiment analysis, these factors can produce unstable attribution behavior across languages even when top-line classification accuracy appears acceptable. This paper asks: \textit{which explanation methods preserve faithfulness under multilingual shift while remaining computationally practical?}

We compare five perturbation-based methods in a unified benchmark and evaluate both global trends and instance-level disagreement. Our contributions are: (i) a compact multilingual benchmark over two model families, (ii) a quantitative analysis of faithfulness--efficiency trade-offs and statistical significance, and (iii) a reproducible case-study pipeline for qualitative grounding of disagreement.

\section{Related Work}
Local post-hoc methods such as LIME \citep{ribeiro2016lime}, SHAP \citep{lundberg2017shap}, and token-removal style analyses \citep{li2016understanding} are widely used in NLP interpretability. However, several works show that attribution quality can degrade under distribution shift and can be confounded by perturbation artifacts \citep{hooker2019roar,atanasova2020diagnostic}. For multilingual settings, the interaction between tokenizer behavior and language-specific morphology is especially important \citep{wu2022multilingual}. Our setup follows this line by jointly evaluating explanation faithfulness, runtime cost, and stability across language-resource conditions.

\section{Model Architectures}
\textbf{mDeBERTa (encoder).} We use a multilingual DeBERTa-style encoder classifier that predicts sentiment labels from contextualized token embeddings.

\textbf{Qwen (decoder).} We use a lightweight decoder-style language model (Qwen-0.5B) wrapped for label-probability output.

\textbf{Explanation methods.} We compare LOO, LIME, SHAP, Marginalization, and PLEX as implemented in the repository. LOO and LIME provide strong perturbation baselines, SHAP approximates additive contributions, Marginalization reduces hard-mask artifacts via context-aware replacement, and PLEX is a perturbation-light proxy.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Data and protocol}
Experiments are run on three languages: English (eng), Afrikaans (afr), and Javanese (jav), representing high-/mid-/low-resource conditions. For each sample, we generate method-specific attributions without retraining the base model.

\subsection{Metrics}
We report NAOPC (faithfulness), elapsed runtime per explanation (efficiency), and token count (length control). NAOPC is normalized for cross-model comparability.

\subsection{Statistical testing}
To compare methods under repeated-measures design, we use Friedman tests over per-instance NAOPC rankings and pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with effect size $r=|z|/\sqrt{n}$.

\subsection{Robustness analysis}
We evaluate: (i) coefficient of variation (CV) of NAOPC across samples, (ii) length sensitivity, and (iii) disagreement range (max--min NAOPC across methods per sentence).

\section{Case Study: Qualitative Grounding Pipeline}
This section operationalizes the Step 4 pipeline to connect aggregate statistics with concrete multilingual examples.

\subsection{Step 4.1: Select extreme cases}
For each language, we select the sample with maximal disagreement range:
\[
\mathrm{Range}(x)=\max_m \mathrm{NAOPC}(x,m)-\min_m \mathrm{NAOPC}(x,m).
\]
In mDeBERTa, the largest ranges are observed at ENG-46 (0.826), AFR-32 (0.334), and JAV-24 (0.334), indicating high method divergence in these texts.

\subsection{Step 4.2: Re-run model predictions}
For selected samples, we keep model parameters fixed and re-run \textit{attribution only} for all methods. This avoids confounding explanation drift with model retraining effects.

\subsection{Step 4.3: Token-level attribution alignment}
We build a token\(\times\)method matrix per sentence (LOO/SHAP/LIME/Marginalization/PLEX), then visualize it as a heatmap. Disagreement regions are tokens with large cross-method variance. This makes divergence inspectable at word level rather than only through global NAOPC.

\subsection{Step 4.4: Model prediction anchoring}
Each attribution table is paired with raw model outputs: logits, predicted class, and class-probability distribution. This anchor clarifies \textit{what} prediction is being explained before comparing \textit{how} methods explain it.

\subsection{Step 4.5: Divergence interpretation}
We interpret disagreement by checking whether methods emphasize different linguistic cues (negation, intensifiers, idiomatic spans, or noisy tokens). This step is crucial for explaining why methods diverge in low-resource or morphologically challenging contexts.

\section{Discussion}
The benchmark shows a clear computational hierarchy: LIME is prohibitively expensive for routine multilingual analysis, while PLEX and Marginalization offer practical alternatives. Faithfulness differences are model-dependent: encoder results exhibit stronger between-method significance than decoder results, suggesting architecture-specific explanation behavior. Cross-lingual patterns indicate that low-resource conditions remain difficult, especially for Qwen.

Limitations include restricted language count, NAOPC-focused faithfulness evaluation, and currently qualitative (rather than fully automated) case-study interpretation. Future work should expand language coverage, add counterfactual checks, and quantify token-level agreement with dedicated alignment metrics.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented an end-to-end multilingual explainability analysis over mDeBERTa and Qwen with five perturbation-based methods. Results confirm a robust faithfulness--efficiency trade-off and non-trivial cross-lingual degradation. The proposed case-study pipeline (extreme-case selection, attribution rerun, token alignment, prediction anchoring, divergence interpretation) provides the narrative layer needed to turn summary statistics into interpretable multilingual evidence.
